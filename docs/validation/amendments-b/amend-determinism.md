## 1 Home items

### Item 20 — one-way data flow; the dossier is the authority (HOME §7.7)

**Landed:** §7.7 (the dossier contract, items 1–8; `Assignment.existence / dossier / pinned / use / knownTo`; the
`Rejection` record; the precedence line), §7.1 (`rejectSite`, `placeAt`, `EditBase.dossier`, `DossierRef`; `delete`
never removes a dossier-backed element; the edit-target rows), §7.2 (dossier-backed existence and positive placement as
protections), §7.3 (item 1's effective scope; item 8; the operator table row), §7.4 (the `assign / unassign` row rewritten;
a `rejectSite / placeAt` row), §5.2 (F-SITING never drops a dossier item; F-EDIT for a blocked placement), §5.3 (every
dossier-backed element live and placed), §5.4 (the report's standing `dossier` list), §6.1 (post-build placements draw on
`siting` keyed by the event), §6.3 (a dossier-backed assignment survives as a fixed input).

**Mechanism in plain words.** The dossier comes in through the host door as ordinary events marked with the dossier's own
element id, and the module never sends anything back that would change the dossier. Everything the dossier names is an
assignment whose *existence* is held: the map can move it, never remove it. Deleting such a thing on the map becomes a
*rejection* of that site — the element is put somewhere else by the one placement solver with the least disturbance, and
the site stays off-limits only until the settlement next changes (an epoch, or a dossier change touching that
neighbourhood), when the hold lapses by itself. "Make this place X" is a positive placement: honoured when the rules allow,
otherwise the user is told which rule blocks it and offered the nearest places that work. Filler the generator invented on
its own may still be deleted, and whatever the dossier needs of it is provided elsewhere.

**Rulings:** J-A20.71 (dossier existence below the lock, above assignments, drawn geometry, rules and defaults; an
unplaceable element is placed with its failed rows reported, never omitted), J-A20.72 (the proposed filler rule adopted),
J-A20.73 (positive placements and map edits honoured as pins before a dossier event's mutation; incompatible ones
orphaned and reported), J-A20.74 (the map delete is `rejectSite`; the lapse is written by the lapsing epoch or dossier
event inside its own touched set, never a log event of its own — replay = session stays exact).

### Item 22 — dossier changes mutate the map, never re-roll it (HOME §7.7)

**Landed:** §7.7 (One path — placement then mutation; re-application with the least-disruption order pointed to §4.7 S10b;
the `DisruptionBudgetRow` table and `EditBase.disruption`; continuity; the epoch clause pointed to §4.11), §6.2 (histories,
not states), §6.5 (9) (the history-determinism test), §7.1 (the dry run computes the disruption against the budget; an
over-budget dossier event still lands), §7.3 item 1 (the budget bounds the effective scope), §7.4 (the epoch row: a
mid-history dossier event is a mutation at that epoch).

**Mechanism in plain words.** A dossier change lands on the map as it stands: the solver tries the least disruptive site
first (empty plots, then under-used ones, then a use that can shift within the neighbourhood, and a join only when the new
thing needs the land); the buildings on the chosen plot are adapted, not re-rolled; a removal simply leaves the plot
vacant. Every such change carries a disruption report — what moved, what was joined or vacated and why — measured against
a table budget that limits how far the search may reach; a dossier change is never refused for being disruptive, only
reported. Because changes are folded in order, the map is a function of the seed and the ordered history: the same history
replays exactly, and two players who reach the same dossier by different routes may rightly hold different maps.

**Rulings:** J-A22.71 (continuity over path-independence), J-A22.72 (a dossier the document is created from enters both S2's
demand and the log's positions 0…k claims-first; later dossier events are mutations), J-A22.73 (the budget bounds the search
and the effective scope, never the dossier's authority; a user's positive placement over budget is F-EDIT).

### Item 23 — emulation-first defaults; no hard-coded universals (HOME §7.6)

**Landed:** §7.6 (the "Emulation first" bullet: the cell-level marker `provenance.source` in §17.1 (6)'s grammar plus an
`extension` field, the parity presets `MFCG` / `FTG` that zero every extension, the reading rule for absolutes, the
engineering-invariant exclusion; the enumeration of editable tables extended to every table the amendments add).

**Mechanism in plain words.** Every rule's default is what MFCG or FTG does; our additions sit on top as tendencies with a
chance or a weight, or as options. Each table cell says which reference behaviour it copies, and a cell that goes beyond
the references is marked as an extension; two presets switch every extension off, which is how the parity tests prove the
principle. Wherever the design says a settlement "always" or "never" does something, that is a default table value that a
region, era or setting may change — only engineering invariants (planarity, "never re-rolled") stay absolute.

**Ruling:** J-A23.71 (the marker lives in §7.6's cell schema, not only in §17.1's provenance values; parity presets are the
proof).

### 8.3 The mechanism in plain words

A building's outside — its wings, height, roof, doors and cellar — keeps the floor the owner set for the geometry (item
31). Its inside follows the people in it. The plan rung reads who lives there (the occupants' band and how many
households) and cuts the interior to a rule table by band, the interior ladder that §2.6 states: one household with big
rooms and small staff rooms when rich; one or two households when middling; a lodging house with a corridor giving each
family its own door, a shared kitchen and a common room when middling-poor; a tenement of one-family apartments off a
corridor when poor; single rooms when destitute. When the occupants change — an epoch's `relet`, a band fall, or the
user's own `relet` — nothing outside the walls moves: the stair, the entrances and the chimneys stay where they are, and
the partitions, corridors and doors are re-cut with the least disturbance, rooms that still fit keeping their rings and
their addresses even when a parlour becomes a family's room. The only new thing a room carries is which household's unit
it belongs to, and that is editable like everything else. Every household gets a unit with its own door, a unit shrinks to
the next poorer form before anyone is left out, and the number of households a shell may take is bounded by what the shell
can hold — so the poor live in the same shells as before, behind the same fronts, in re-partitioned interiors, and only
the surplus beyond a shell's capacity goes to the cheap stock, back courts and hidden plots the other items provide.

### 8.4 The correction (f) forced in my own landed design — J-A28.71 amended

My landed §7.3 row derived a stale plan "with the cached result as `prior`", while my landed §6.2 says the cache "can be
dropped to `absent` at any time without loss" and my §6.5 (9) test demands identical plans in any click order. Those three
cannot all hold once a building's inputs change more than once between clicks — exactly what (f) makes ordinary (a
`relet` at one epoch, a band fall at the next, the user clicking only later). With the cache as prior, a user who clicked
after each change would see rich → middle → poor, one who clicked once would see rich → poor cut directly, and a host that
had dropped its cache would see a third plan. The route's item 28 (f) section names this (ruling 5) and recommends the
fixed chain. Ruled here, because purity and click-order identity are this part's invariants: **the prior is committed
state, never the cache** — the stored plan where the user has edited the plan, else the as-built plan (the class's
programme at the plot's `builtBand` row with the template's `households.built`) carried along §2.6's interior ladder one
row or one household at a time, each step a least-disruption mutation whose draws are keyed by the step. The cache then
holds any step already derived and is only a memo. Re-uniting the households returns to the as-built plan exactly, by
construction, with no inverse-mutation bookkeeping. The second half of the ruling (J-A28.72) closes the lifecycle path a
click cannot take: a STORED plan is model data, and a click logs nothing, so a stored plan whose inputs change is re-cut
inside the event that changes them — the `relet`, the epoch, the designation mutation — recorded in `EditBase.mutations`
and listed as a fate, exactly as S11's mutator already treats a stored site plan (J-A18.71); an unstored plan simply goes
stale and waits for the click.

Agreed, and it is the right shape for this design: the stock outlives its occupants (item 31), so the shell is the floor
and the interior is a function of who lives there. Three things I add from the determinism side. First, the interior must
be *derived*, never *remembered*: two people who open the same building at different moments, or after one of them
cleared a cache, must see the same tenement — so the plan is always cut from the building's as-built interior along the
ladder to its current occupants, never from whatever happened to be on screen last (§8.4). Second, "where do the poor
live" has a second half: a shell holds only so many single rooms, so the household count a building takes is capped by
its capacity, and the surplus goes to the cheap stock, the back courts and the hidden plots of items 30 and 31 — the
design now has both halves, and a count never silently exceeds what a building can hold. Third, your last sentence ("the
more rooms for more occupying families the richer a building gets") reads best as a property of the shell — a mansion
holds more families than a cottage when both are let out — rather than of the occupants, which is how it is built in.
One consequence to know about: a floor plan you have edited by hand is re-cut around your edits when its occupants
change; you will see that listed as a consequence of the epoch, never as a silent change.
