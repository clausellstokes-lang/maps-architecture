**Where.** §4.11 (REPLACE: the Survivors rule rewritten as the wealth ladder with the geometry floor, the epoch's order,

not repeated; §10.3's re-letting line is the assignments seat's APPEND.

**Mechanism, in plain words.** When an epoch moves a block's prosperity past a band cut (by more than the hysteresis),
the block is marked "due" and nothing else happens at once. The wealth ladder then acts on that block's standing stock
over time in three rungs: first, who lives there and how the fabric fares (a mansion let out to several households, its
rooms re-cut when next clicked, sheds in the yard, the garden built over, the roof patched — or, growing rich, the
reverse); second, after a table duration, how the holding is held (a back court behind the house reached by a passage,
or a court cleared; a big house changing class but keeping its shell); and only third, when a plot is vacant, ruined,
struck by a destructive event or redeveloped, its geometry — split into row houses or joined with worn neighbours and
rebuilt — by S10's join-and-split law re-run for that block with its neighbours fixed. No occupied building is ever
demolished by a band change; a redevelopment first closes or relocates the uses, then vacates, then re-cuts. The speed
is asymmetric: growing rich reaches the geometry rung within a few epochs because cheap stock is replaceable; growing
poor waits for vacancy or ruin because expensive stock is not — the ladder table's durations are keyed by direction and
by the stock's `replaceability` (a column of the class table's template rows, read at the band the stock was built for),
and churn caps per district and per block keep every epoch small. The geometry floor: every plot remembers the band its
stock was built for (`builtBand`, with the value `builtProsperity`); its geometry can never read below its floor — the
settlement's middle band for stock built at or above it, its own band below — by wealth change alone, so a mansion
district grown poor reads as middle-class geometry with poor occupants, condition and names, and it cycles freely
between rich and middle through rungs 1 and 2; only a rung-3 event, a hand edit of that kind, an authorial re-derivation
(brush, band edit, Adjust) or a completed upward refurbishment rewrites the record. Fused plots keep their largest
parent's lineage and split children the parent's plus an index, so the seed survives and an undo is exact. With no epoch
nothing runs, so the static base is unchanged.

**Rulings** (index rows in the blocks file; full entries in RATIONALE-A): J-A31.41 the ladder replaces the Survivors rule
(direction fixed, occupied buildings protected, operators reused); J-A31.42 replaceability per class × band, read at

1. It is the right answer, and it is what real cities did: the grand houses of a district that fell out of fashion were

   Rome's insulae — and the street never showed it; only the interiors did. The design's realism tiebreaker favours it,
   and it costs no new records: the room programme already took households as an input, and the ladder's rows are table
   rows.
2. The poor therefore end up in three kinds of place, and that is what makes a poor district look different from a
   poor-occupied rich district: re-cut interiors behind grand fronts (this rule); back courts and rear tenements reached
   by a passage behind them (item 30, rung 2); and cheap replaceable stock at the edge and in districts that were poor
   from the start (the shanty rule, small plots). The map reads the difference in the plots; the plan reads it in the
   walls.

   occupants it contradicts "more prosperity means a larger home for a smaller family"; read on the building it is
   capacity — the richer a house was built, the more families it can hold when let out — and that is how it is recorded

4. What stays fixed in a re-cut: the outer walls, the stair, the street and yard doors, the chimneys — so the house looks
   the same from the street and the chimneys still sit over hearths. What moves: partition walls, corridors, doors and
   room names. Both directions work, and an undo is exact.
5. Nobody is left unhoused: a family's apartment shrinks to a single room before any family is dropped, and a house
   takes no more families than its floor area holds; when a district's population falls, a lodging house loses families
   one by one and is vacated only when the last leaves.
6. One consequence to know: a rich district that grows poor does not gain front doors on the street — the extra families
   enter through the one street door and a stair hall — so at map zoom the change shows only through condition (worn
   roofs, patched walls), yard infill and, after the table's years, back courts. If you want the street to show it
   sooner, that is a table value (the condition rows and the rung-2 durations), not a design change.
7. Common rooms, shared kitchens and the cooking place are programme rows, so a culture or an era can keep a common
   kitchen down to the poor row or drop it earlier.
